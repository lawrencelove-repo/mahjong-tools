Setup

  • Players: Four, seated at a square table. Each seat is a wind: East (dealer), South, West, North, clockwise.
  • Tiles: 136 tiles — three suits (man / pin / sou, 1–9 × 4) plus honors (four winds and three dragons, × 4 each). No flowers, seasons, or jokers. If your set has extras, remove them. Optional: one red five per suit (aka dora).
  • Wall: Shuffle, build four walls of 17 stacks × 2 tiles high (34 tiles per wall). Push walls together into a square.
  • Deal: After the dice break (below), each player gets 13 tiles; the dealer (East) draws a 14th and plays first. Tiles are usually dealt in batches of four, counter-clockwise, starting with East, until everyone has 12; then one more each to 13; East takes one extra from the wall.
  • Dead wall: The last 14 tiles of the wall (7 stacks) are the rinshan / dead wall. Dora indicators sit on it; replacements for kans come from here. Play continues from the live wall until it runs out (draw / exhaustive draw).
  • Round structure: An East round, then South (and often West/North in longer games). Within a round, the dealer seat rotates after non-dealer wins or certain draws; dealer stays if they win or if everyone draws with the dealer in tenpai (table rules vary slightly).

Breaking the wall with dice

Two dice decide which wall is broken and where on that wall the deal begins. East rolls both dice and uses the total (2–12).

  1. Which wall: Starting with East’s own wall as 1, count walls counter-clockwise (East → North → West → South → East…) until you reach the dice total. That player’s wall is the break wall. (Equivalently: total mod 4, with a multiple of 4 landing on East’s wall.)
  2. Where on that wall: Sit behind the chosen wall (or imagine looking at it from that seat). From the right-hand end of that wall, count the same number of stacks (pairs of tiles, top+bottom together) as the dice total.
  3. Split: Break after that stack. The next stack continuing around the square (the live side) is where dealing starts. Work counter-clockwise along the walls as you deal.
  4. Dead wall: Set aside the 7 stacks (14 tiles) at the other side of the break as the dead wall. Flip the end indicator for dora once the deal is done (table timing varies slightly).

Example: East rolls 7. Count walls 1=East, 2=North, 3=West, 4=South, 5=East, 6=North, 7=West — break West’s wall. From West’s right end, count 7 stacks and split there; deal from the next stack onward. The 7 stacks on the far side of the split become the dead wall.

Point sticks, wind marker & compass

Many sets include scoring sticks and wind accessories. They are optional if you track scores on paper or an app, but they are the traditional way to pay and to show whose turn and which round it is.

Point sticks (tenbou)

Players start with a fixed score (commonly 25,000 or 30,000). Sticks represent that score in four common denominations. There is no number printed on them — classic hand sticks are white with red and black circles. (Automatic-table sets often use solid colors instead: white 100, blue 1,000, yellow 5,000, red 10,000.)

Exact dot layouts vary by maker; “more red / busier = higher value” is the usual rule of thumb. A 25,000 start is often dealt as 1×10,000 + 2×5,000 + 4×1,000 + 10×100.

When someone wins, losers hand over sticks for the scored amount. You can make change among yourselves so each person keeps a usable mix of values.

  • Riichi bet: When you declare riichi, place a 1,000-point stick on the table (toward the center). That stick sits as a pot. The eventual winner of the hand collects all riichi sticks currently on the table (including their own if they win). If the hand is a draw, riichi sticks usually stay for the next hand’s winner (table rules).
  • Honba (counters): When the dealer stays for another hand (renchan) — after a dealer win, or after a draw where the dealer is tenpai under common rules — place a 100-point stick near the dealer (or on the wind marker) for each consecutive stay. Each honba adds a small bonus to the next win (typically +300 to the winner on ron, or +100 from each player on tsumo). Clear the sticks when the dealer finally rotates.
  • Tenpai / noten after a draw: See Winning for how sticks settle when the wall runs out.

Wind marker (round-wind indicator)

A small block or tile shows the prevailing / round wind (bakaze): East for the first round, then South (and West/North in longer games). Seat winds are personal (who is dealer East this hand); the marker is shared and only changes when the whole table advances to the next round.

Place it where everyone can see it — often beside the dead wall or at the center. When East round ends and South round begins, turn the marker to South. Combined with knowing who is currently dealer, everyone can tell which wind tiles score as “seat wind” vs “round wind.”

Compass (optional)

A table compass (or wind dial) sits in the middle and orients the four seat winds so newcomers can see East / South / West / North at a glance. Some compasses also display the round wind or hold the honba / riichi sticks.

Important: mahjong seat order is not the same as a geographic compass. Going counter-clockwise around the table, seats run East → North → West → South. Going clockwise, they run East → South → West → North. Use the compass as a seating aid, not as true north.

When the dealer rotates, either rotate the compass so the new East faces the new dealer, or leave the compass fixed and remember that “East” on the dial means the current dealer seat — pick one convention and stick to it.

Names & terms

Man / pin / sou
Characters, circles, and bamboo suits (often called craks, dots, and bams in English).
Honors
Winds (East, South, West, North) and dragons (white / haku, green / hatsu, red / chun).
Meld / set
A group of three (or four for a kan): a pon (triplet), chi (sequence in one suit), or kan (quad).
Pair
Two identical tiles; every standard winning hand includes one pair.
Call
Claiming a discarded tile to complete a meld: chi (only from the player on your left), pon, kan, or ron (win on discard).
Tsumo / ron
Win by self-draw (tsumo) or on another’s discard (ron).
Tenpai / noten
Ready hand (one tile from winning) vs. not ready. Matters at exhaustive draws.
Riichi
A declaration when you are closed (no open melds except from your own kans) and tenpai: pay a 1,000-point stick, lock your hand, and gain scoring benefits if you win.
Dora
Bonus tiles indicated by the dead-wall indicator (the next tile in sequence, wrapping 9→1 and honor cycles). Red fives and ura-dora after a riichi win also add value.
Yaku
Named hand patterns. You need at least one yaku to win (dora alone is not enough).
Furiten
You cannot ron if a winning tile is among your own discards (or under certain riichi/tenpai restrictions). You can still tsumo.
Point sticks / tenbou
Physical counters for score, riichi bets, honba, and tenpai/noten payments.
Honba
Consecutive dealer-stay counter; usually marked with 100-point sticks and adds a small bonus to the next win.
Bakaze / seat wind
Round wind (shared, on the wind marker) vs. your personal seat wind this hand (East is always the dealer).
River / discard pile
Your face-up discards in order — used for calls, defense, and furiten.

Turns & turn order

Play proceeds counter-clockwise from the dealer (East → North → West → South → East… in wind-seat terms; visually left around the table from East in standard seating diagrams).

  1. Draw one tile from the live wall (or a dead-wall replacement after declaring a kan).
  2. Optional: declare a closed kan, add to an open pon (shouminkan), declare riichi (if eligible), or win by tsumo.
  3. Discard one tile face-up into your discard river. Discards are kept in order so furiten and calls are clear.
  4. Other players may call that discard (chi / pon / kan / ron) before the next player draws. Priority: ron over pon/kan over chi. If nobody calls, the next player draws.

After an open meld, the caller discards and play continues from them (the skipped players do not take a normal turn). Winning ends the hand immediately.

Discards (the river)

In riichi, discards are public information and are watched closely. Each player builds a neat river in front of themselves — usually left-to-right in rows of six — so the order of every discard stays visible for the whole hand.

  • Do not reshuffle or hide discards. Later tiles go after earlier ones. If you called a meld, the claimed tile is taken from someone’s river (or marked), leaving a gap or a clear memory of what left.
  • Defense: Opponents read which tiles are “safe” (already discarded, or matching honor/terminals that look dead) before dealing into a riichi or a threatening hand.
  • Furiten: If any tile that would complete your hand already appears in your river, you are furiten and cannot ron — only tsumo. Temporary furiten also applies if you pass on a winning discard and then try to ron a different tile before your next draw (common rule).
  • Riichi discard: The tile you discard when declaring riichi is often turned sideways in the river so everyone can see when you riichi’d and which tile was the declaration discard.
  • Calls: Chi / pon / kan / ron all refer to the latest discard. Because rivers are ordered, it is obvious whose tile was claimed and whether a win tile was ever thrown earlier.

Good habit: pause a beat after every discard so others can call, then the next player draws. Rushing draws is a common source of missed calls and arguments.

Hand formation overview

A standard win is four melds + one pair (14 tiles when complete). Melds may be closed (formed from your draws) or opened by calling. Sequences must be three consecutive tiles in one suit; triplets/quads are identical tiles.

Special shapes exist (seven pairs, thirteen orphans) and still require a valid yaku. Keep your hand secret until you open a meld or win. Open melds are laid face-up; closed tiles stay concealed.

Win conditions

  • Complete a valid hand shape and at least one yaku (or a yakuman pattern).
  • Win by tsumo (draw the winning tile) or ron (claim a discard), unless furiten blocks ron.
  • After a win, score using han/fu (or yakuman). Pay with sticks (or write it down). The winner also takes any riichi sticks on the table, and honba bonuses if counters are out.
  • Then deal the next hand according to dealer rotation and round wind rules.

Exhaustive draw (ryuukyoku) and tenpai sticks

If the live wall runs out and nobody has won after the last discard, the hand is a draw. Players then declare tenpai (ready) or noten (not ready), usually in seat order starting with East. Anyone who declared riichi must show their hand. Others who claim tenpai show enough of the hand to prove it; you may voluntarily say noten even if you are tenpai (to hide your waits).

Noten players pay a shared 3,000-point pool to the tenpai players (paid with sticks):

  • 1 tenpai / 3 noten: each noten pays 1,000 to the tenpai player (tenpai gains 3,000).
  • 2 tenpai / 2 noten: each noten pays 1,500; each tenpai receives 1,500.
  • 3 tenpai / 1 noten: the noten player pays 1,000 to each tenpai (pays 3,000 total).
  • All tenpai or all noten: no exchange.

Shape-only tenpai (ready but without a yaku yet) still counts as tenpai for this payment. After settling, check whether the dealer was tenpai: under common tenpai renchan rules the dealer stays and a honba stick is added; if the dealer was noten, seats rotate. Riichi sticks left on the table usually carry to the next hand.
